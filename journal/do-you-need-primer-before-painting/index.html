Do you need primer before painting?

By the Painter Hotline crew · Updated October 30, 2026 · 7 min read

Sometimes, and when you do, the kind matters as much as the decision. Here’s how our crews decide, surface by surface.

Quick answerYou don’t need primer on clean, sound, previously painted walls in a similar color. You do need it on bare drywall and patches, stains, glossy or slick surfaces, raw wood and knots, and when changing color dramatically. Each of those calls for a different type of primer.

What primer actually does

Primer solves surface problems that paint isn’t designed for: uneven absorption, poor grip on slick finishes, stains that bleed through, and tannins rising out of wood. Paint is formulated to look good and wear well. Primer is formulated to make the surface ready for it. Fine Homebuilding calls priming “the final step of any painting prep,” which is how we treat it too.

When you need primer (and which one)

SurfaceWhyUsual primer
New drywallPaper and joint compound absorb differently, so the finish looks blotchyPVA or drywall primer-sealer
Patches and repairsPatched spots absorb differently and show through as dull halosDrywall primer (works on bare and previously painted areas)
Marker, ink, tarSolvent-type stains bleed into fresh paintWater-based stain-blocking primer
Nicotine, food, oil-based stainsWater-based primers struggle to block themOil-based or shellac-based primer
Knots and raw woodTannins bleed yellow-brown through latexShellac (knots) or slow-dry oil primer (bare wood)
Glossy paint, laminate, tilePaint can’t grip slick surfaces and peelsScuff-sand plus a high-bond primer
Big color changeOld color shows through, adding coatsTinted primer (gray for deep colors)
Peeling or alligatored paintNew paint can only be as sound as what’s under itScrape and sand, then a peel-bonding primer

The primer types and uses above follow Fine Homebuilding’s primer guide (linked below). Product choice still depends on the label and the topcoat, so we read both.

When you can skip it

If the walls are already painted, clean, dull rather than glossy, free of stains and repairs, and you’re staying in roughly the same color family, a good paint goes straight on. That’s most bedroom repaints. “Paint and primer in one” products work well here. They aren’t a replacement for a dedicated primer-sealer on bare drywall or a stain blocker on a water mark.

Spot-priming versus full priming

A wall with a handful of patches usually gets spot-primed over each repair. If there are many repairs, or the wall will get a sheen above flat, we often prime the whole wall so it absorbs evenly and the patches don’t flash through the finish. On walls with critical side light, primer won’t hide a poor tape joint; that’s a surface repair job first.

Older homes

In homes built before 1978, sanding or scraping old paint can disturb lead-based paint. In Colorado, work that disturbs painted surfaces in that housing triggers pre-renovation education (the Renovate Right pamphlet at least 7 days before work) and lead-safe work practices. We plan for that before prep starts. See how we prep older homes.

What to ask your painter

  • Which surfaces will be primed, and with what?
  • Will repairs be spot-primed or will whole walls be primed?
  • How will glossy trim be prepared?
  • Is the primer tinted for the color change?

If the answers are specific, you’re talking to someone who prepares properly. That’s one of our nine standards.

Questions people ask

Do you need primer before painting walls?

Not always. Clean, sound, previously painted walls in a similar color usually don't need it. You do need primer on bare drywall and patches, stains, glossy or slick surfaces, raw wood, and when making a big color change.

Is paint and primer in one enough?

On sound, previously painted walls it often is. It isn't a substitute for a primer-sealer on bare drywall, a stain-blocking primer on water or smoke stains, or a bonding primer on glossy trim.

What primer covers water stains?

A stain-blocking primer. Water-based primers handle solvent-type marks such as marker, while oil-based or shellac-based primers are the usual choice for tougher stains such as nicotine, food or oil-based stains. Fix the leak first.

Do you need primer to go from a dark color to a light color?

Often yes. A primer evens out the old color so the new one can cover in fewer coats. Going light to dark, a gray-tinted primer helps the deep color cover.

How soon do you paint after priming?

Follow the product label for recoat time, and don't leave primer uncoated for long; Fine Homebuilding notes primers should be topcoated within a couple of days.
